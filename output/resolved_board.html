My read of the three tickets

I can see the design tradeoff in every tab: selective delegation kept the desk readable, but the run stopped at evidence and money controls because the system has no fulfillment tools. The audit trail records Boss’s bounded handoffs; the Actual sections show exactly where those handoffs stopped.

101 — useful financial progress, incomplete fulfillment. The Expected tab says Inventory should establish the physical constraint before Accounting reviews invoice 501. Actual matches that sequence: size-S stock is zero, the shortfall is one, and the $840 invoice payment was approved and recorded. The difference is visible in the Actual text: there is no shipment, receipt, or new stock record. Paying first protects the vendor relationship, but it also spends cash before the customer order can be confirmed. I would keep Inventory first, then use one Inventory agent with the context and cash tools; Customer Service could wait until a receipt date exists.

102 — the cleanest success. The Expected tab calls Facilities first, then Accounting. Actual follows that path and the $2,400 rent payment is recorded. The ticket is the only one marked resolved because the requested business action is the payment itself. This shows the benefit of a narrow route: Inventory and Customer Service were not pulled in. It also shows a tradeoff: a single Facilities agent could have produced the same recommendation, but the separate Accounting approval makes the money boundary easier to audit.

103 — safe refusal, unfinished customer outcome. Expected says Inventory checks quantity and Accounting evaluates cost and margin. Actual confirms 8 available, a shortfall of 12, and a $264 purchase need. The approval history shows a human approved the request, while the Cash tab shows why execution still stopped: after tickets 101 and 102, only $160 remained. Refusing $264 prevented a negative balance, but the design gives no next-best action such as partial fulfillment or a ranked cash-priority recommendation. Customer Service was routed early even though it could not draft a reliable promise yet; that is a small efficiency cost of the broader customer-care route.

What the visible evidence changes in my design

The ticket tabs show that the agents are strongest at reading structured facts, delegating selectively, and preparing approval decisions. The Cash tab proves the safety tradeoff: $3,400 − $840 − $2,400 = $160, and the third payment was refused. I would preserve the two-step human approval plus atomic cash check. I would also label an approval as “approved but not executed” so ticket 103 cannot look complete merely because a human clicked approve.

With today’s tools, I could solve another stock check, rent or vendor obligation review, and margin-aware bulk-order assessment. I could not complete vendor shipment/receiving, send or track a customer message, or record an authorized discount sale. The Actual sections expose those gaps: 101 has no receipt, 101 and 103 have no sent message, and 103 has no price-override transaction. I would add a Vendor Operations agent with purchase-order and receiving tools, a draft-and-delivery-status communications tool with a no-send safeguard, and a Pricing agent with discount approval, margin, and approved-price fields.
